00.
Simple Host for companies

Give everyone in the company a place to build.

Someone signs in with your company account, describes a page to the AI agent they already use, and it is live at their own address a minute later. No ticket, no repository, no one to ask.

You run it. One service on your own Kubernetes cluster, against your own database, your own storage and your own identity provider — on whichever cloud you are already on. Nothing leaves your network, and every change and every visit is written down.

01.

What a person gets

An address of their own, and somewhere for a page to keep what it collects.

Their own hostname

Every person gets one label under your base domain, so their pages sit at an address that is theirs rather than a path on a shared one. A page they publish runs on their origin and no colleague's.

Pages that hold data

A folder of files, a JSON document the page reads and writes without a schema, and an asset store for the things that are too big to sit in a document. Enough for a sign-up form, a poll, a project board that pulls from Jira and draws its own timeline.

The agent does it

Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Copilot. They install one skill, or connect to the instance's MCP endpoint and reach every operation as a tool — publish, roll back, share, search, manage a team.

Nothing to learn

Signing in is your normal company sign-in. There is no second password, no API key to keep somewhere, and no account for anyone to create or approve.

02.

Who can see what

A page is visible to everyone signed in, or to the people named on it. That choice belongs to whoever made it.

Restricted pages

Name the people who may read it and the page moves to an address of its own. Everyone else gets a 404 — not a sign-in prompt, not a permission error. Somebody who should not know it exists cannot tell that it does.

Editors

Someone else can be given the right to publish to a site without owning it, and the right can be taken back. The audit trail records which of them did what.

Teams

Group people once and share with the group, rather than maintaining the same list on eleven pages.

Leavers

Access follows your identity provider. When somebody's company account stops working, so does everything here — sessions included, not just future sign-ins.

03.

What it writes down

Every change and every visit, kept where you can read them back.

The action log records who published, who rolled back, who was given access to what, and who took it away. The access log records what was visited and by whom. An owner reads their own; an admin reads all of it; both export.

Both are partitioned and pruned on a schedule you set, by a job that runs under the database's owning credential rather than the application's own — so the service that writes the audit trail is not the service that can delete it.

04.

What it runs on

Your cluster, your Postgres, your object storage, your identity provider. The package is the manifests to do it.

Kubernetes

Kustomize manifests: a base, and overlays for bringing your own managed services or running everything in-cluster to evaluate it. Any conformant cluster — managed or your own. Ingress and certificates are the cluster's normal ones.

Postgres and object storage

Any managed Postgres, and your cloud's object storage — Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, Oracle Object Storage, MinIO or anything else that speaks the same API. The service refuses to start unless the database connection verifies the server's certificate in full and the storage endpoint is HTTPS, unless you deliberately opt out to evaluate it locally.

Your identity provider

Anything that speaks OIDC. Which claim marks an administrator, and which email domains may sign in at all, are yours to set. There is no master key anywhere in the system — an admin is a real person who signed in.

Least privilege

The service connects to the database as a role that cannot alter the schema. Migrations run as the owner, separately, as their own step.

Nothing that identifies an installation has a default. If a required setting is missing the process will not start, and it tells you which ones.

05.

Upgrading, backing up, getting it back

Upgrades

The schema is a numbered chain, applied by an init container before the new version serves. Rolling forward is deploying the new image; the migration runs itself.

Backups

A scheduled job syncs every site's assets to your own storage, written with server-side encryption. You can add a key of your own so objects are encrypted before they ever leave the cluster, which puts a leaked backup out of reach without it.

Restore

A command that rebuilds a site and its assets from that storage. It is part of the binary, not a runbook someone has to write.

Checking it works

A smoke test ships with it: signs in, moves a session between hosts, publishes, restricts a site, reads and writes state and assets. Run it after every rollout.

06.

Install it

A local cluster first, on your own machine, in about five minutes.

Docker Desktop with Kubernetes turned on, or minikube. One command brings up ingress, certificates, Postgres, storage and a test identity provider alongside the app, and prints two accounts to sign in with. When you have seen it work, the same manifests point at your real database, storage and provider.

The package documents every setting and what happens when it is wrong, gives a checklist per cloud for the pieces a real install needs, and ships its security review: the threat model, what closes each finding, the penetration tests that were run against it, and the limitations that are accepted rather than solved.
